# Estimating Treatment Effects on the Probability of Condorcet Winners in Randomly Formed Groups

## Experimental Setup

Individuals are randomly assigned to either:

- **Control**: individuals submit a ranking of a fixed set of options.
- **Treatment**: individuals receive a treatment and then submit a ranking of the same options.

No explicit groups are formed during data collection. Each observation is an individual ranking.

The substantive objective is to estimate whether treatment changes the likelihood that a randomly formed group of six individuals admits a **Condorcet winner**.

---

# 1. Estimand

Let:

- \(R_i\) denote the ranking submitted by individual \(i\),
- \(h(R_1,\dots,R_6)\in\{0,1\}\) be an indicator equal to 1 if the six rankings admit a Condorcet winner and 0 otherwise.

Define:

\[
\theta_a
=
\Pr\left(
h(R_1,\dots,R_6)=1
\right),
\qquad a\in\{0,1\},
\]

where the six rankings are drawn independently from the ranking distribution in arm \(a\).

Interpretation:

- \(\theta_0\): probability that a random six-person control group has a Condorcet winner,
- \(\theta_1\): probability that a random six-person treated group has a Condorcet winner.

The treatment effect of interest is:

\[
\tau = \theta_1 - \theta_0.
\]

This quantity measures how treatment changes the probability that a randomly formed six-person group admits a Condorcet winner.

---

# 2. Sample Estimator

Suppose arm \(a\) contains \(n_a\) individuals.

For every unordered six-person subset \(S\subseteq I_a\), compute:

\[
h_S
=
h\big((R_i)_{i\in S}\big).
\]

The estimator for arm \(a\) is:

\[
\hat\theta_a
=
\frac{1}{\binom{n_a}{6}}
\sum_{S:|S|=6} h_S.
\]

The estimated treatment effect is:

\[
\hat\tau
=
\hat\theta_1-\hat\theta_0.
\]

Important:

- use **combinations**, not permutations;
- order within a group of six is irrelevant.

---

# 3. Computing Condorcet Winners

For each six-person group:

1. Aggregate pairwise majority comparisons across all rankings.
2. Construct the induced majority relation.
3. A Condorcet winner exists if one option defeats every other option in pairwise majority voting.
4. If any pairwise contest ties (e.g. 3–3), define whether ties invalidate Condorcet existence. The cleanest convention is typically:
   - ties imply “no Condorcet winner.”

---

# 4. Why Ordinary Standard Errors Are Invalid

The \(\binom{n_a}{6}\) groups are highly dependent because individuals appear repeatedly across many groups.

Therefore:

- the effective sample size is approximately the number of individuals,
- not the number of six-person groups.

Naïve binomial standard errors treating six-person groups as independent observations are incorrect and severely underestimate uncertainty.

---

# 5. Fisher Randomization Inference

Inference is based directly on the experimental randomization.

## Null Hypothesis

Sharp null:

\[
H_0:
\text{treatment has no effect on any individual ranking}.
\]

Under the null, rankings are fixed and only treatment labels are random.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1: Compute observed statistic

Compute:

\[
\hat\tau_{\text{obs}}.
\]

---

### Step 2: Generate permutation distribution

Repeatedly:

1. Randomly reshuffle treatment labels according to the original randomization rule,
2. Preserve the original number treated and control,
3. Recompute:

\[
\hat\tau^{(b)}.
\]

Repeat for \(b=1,\dots,B\).

---

### Step 3: Compute p-value

Two-sided p-value:

\[
p
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^B
1\left(
|\hat\tau^{(b)}|
\ge
|\hat\tau_{\text{obs}}|
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

For a one-sided alternative, use the corresponding tail.

---

# 6. Interpretation of Fisher Inference

This procedure provides:

- exact or asymptotically exact inference under the randomization design,
- valid p-values without requiring analytic variance formulas,
- inference aligned directly with the experimental assignment mechanism.

This is especially attractive when the statistic is nonlinear and combinatorial.

---

# 7. Monte Carlo Power Analysis

Because the estimator is complex and standard analytic power formulas are difficult, power analysis is conducted via simulation.

The objective is to estimate:

\[
\Pr(p \le \alpha),
\]

where \(p\) is the Fisher randomization p-value generated by the planned analysis procedure.

---

# 8. Data-Generating Process

Power calculations require assumptions about the distribution of individual rankings.

Define:

- \(P_0(r)\): probability distribution of rankings under control,
- \(P_1(r)\): probability distribution under treatment.

These can be estimated from pilot data or specified structurally.

---

# 9. Defining Effect Size

Effect size may be defined in two ways.

## Option A: Directly on the estimand

Specify target differences such as:

\[
\tau \in \{0.03,0.05,0.08\}.
\]

Interpretation:

- treatment increases the probability of a Condorcet winner by 3, 5, or 8 percentage points.

---

## Option B: Through ranking distributions

Specify how treatment changes the distribution of individual rankings, then compute the implied value of \(\tau\).

This approach is more structural but more assumption-dependent.

---

# 10. Monte Carlo Power Procedure

For each candidate sample size \(N\):

## Step 1: Generate synthetic experiment

Draw:

- \(n_0\) rankings from \(P_0\),
- \(n_1\) rankings from \(P_1\).

---

## Step 2: Compute treatment statistic

Compute:

\[
\hat\tau
=
\hat\theta_1-\hat\theta_0.
\]

---

## Step 3: Run Fisher randomization inference

Using the simulated data:

1. repeatedly permute treatment labels,
2. recompute \(\hat\tau^{(b)}\),
3. compute permutation p-value.

---

## Step 4: Record rejection

Define:

\[
D_m
=
1[p_m\le \alpha].
\]

---

## Step 5: Repeat many times

Repeat the entire simulated experiment \(M\) times.

Estimated power:

\[
\widehat{\text{Power}}
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{m=1}^M D_m.
\]

---

# 11. Recommended Simulation Structure

Typical choices:

- outer simulations (experiments): \(M=1000\) or more,
- inner permutations per experiment: \(B=500\) to \(2000\).

---

# 12. Computational Approximation

Exact enumeration of all:

\[
\binom{n_a}{6}
\]

groups may become computationally expensive.

A practical approximation is:

- randomly sample many six-person groups within each arm,
- estimate \(\hat\theta_a\) using the sampled groups.

With sufficiently many sampled groups, approximation error becomes negligible relative to experimental uncertainty.

---

# 13. Recommended Empirical Workflow

## Pilot Stage

1. Collect pilot rankings,
2. estimate empirical ranking distributions,
3. compute pilot-implied values of:
   - \(\hat\theta_0\),
   - \(\hat\theta_1\),
   - \(\hat\tau\).

---

## Planning Stage

For a grid of sample sizes and effect sizes:

1. simulate experiments,
2. run the exact planned Fisher procedure,
3. estimate rejection probabilities,
4. construct power curves.

---

## Final Analysis Stage

1. Compute observed treatment effect,
2. conduct Fisher randomization inference,
3. report:
   - estimated treatment effect,
   - permutation p-value,
   - confidence intervals if desired.

---

# 14. Conceptual Interpretation

The experiment randomizes treatment at the individual level, but the outcome of interest is a nonlinear group-level property induced by the distribution of rankings.

The inferential framework therefore treats:

- rankings as individual-level primitive outcomes,
- Condorcet existence as an emergent property of randomly formed groups.

The resulting estimator is a combinatorial functional of the empirical ranking distribution, and Fisher randomization inference provides a natural design-based inferential framework for testing treatment effects on that functional.
